Too Late to Test Before Black Friday? One Day on the Free Tier Answers It

One 22-page document, three passes, three free tiers, and four numbers written down as you work. What the test caught on November 21, where each free tier broke, and what to do if you have already paid.

You have five days to Thanksgiving and six to Black Friday, which is more than enough to find out whether you should buy an AI plan at all. The test is one of your own documents, three passes, about 90 minutes.

Thanksgiving is Thursday, November 26, and Black Friday is November 27. Six days out, the useful move is not a deal list. It is one task you actually have to finish, run three times on three free tiers, with four numbers written down as you go.

I ran it on November 21, 2026 with a 22-page supplier agreement, the least festive document I had, because the reader about to spend $200 on a year of AI is rarely shopping for help with a menu. Three free tiers, three passes each, and they failed in three different ways.

Six Days Is Not the Problem

The deadline feels closer than it is. On November 18 neither OpenAI nor Anthropic listed a holiday price on its US pricing page, and Claude's $200 annual option is available in July as readily as in November. The year-up-front arithmetic lands on the same number in any month. Waiting until Friday gets you what you can pay today, and the deal you are afraid of missing is a discount nobody posted.

The one real deadline is the annual commitment. A $20 plan billed monthly is $240 a year and can be stopped in a month. The same plan billed annually is $200 and becomes a decision you make once. The $40 it saves is small. The twelve months it buys are not.

Write each one down while you work. Recalled numbers flatter.

Those four separate the two reasons people pay. Capacity is the one a plan reliably fixes, because the ceiling lifts when the payment clears and the work finishes. Quality is the one that may not move at all, since the next tier up is a faster version of the same model reading the same ambiguous document. Spend on capacity. Treat a quality problem as a reason to try another tool, not a bigger tier.

Run It in Three Passes

Pass one is the task itself: your real document, one prompt, no coaching, no retries after the first answer. Pass two is the correction round, where you point at the worst thing in the draft and watch how the tool handles being told. Pass three is the export, because a memo you cannot move into your own files is a demo, not work.

Spread the passes across a day rather than an afternoon. The thing that breaks between them is the message ceiling, and a ceiling you never touch is a ceiling you did not need to pay to raise. ChatGPT free hit one on my run at 9:41 a.m. and would not accept another message until 6:52 p.m. That is the argument for testing across a day in one paragraph.

A confident memo built on half a document is neither, and I only caught it because pass two asked for page numbers. Whatever you test, ask for citations. They are the cheapest lie detector in the protocol, and the document tests we run elsewhere use the same trick for the same reason.

The Fallback Plan

If Thursday morning arrives and you still have not decided, buy nothing this week. Run two free tiers in parallel on the same task and split it by job: draft in one, check the other. Two accounts at $0 cover a single document more comfortably than the two paid plans at $39.99, and they answer the only question that matters on a deadline, which is whether the work gets finished at all.

Buy on Friday if the same wall hits twice in one week. One wall on a bad afternoon is a prompt you can fix. Two walls on ordinary work is a subscription you will use, and by then the annual price is the right one because the usage is already proven.

If You Already Bought

An annual plan bought before you did this is not a disaster if you spend the first 24 hours properly. Give it the hardest task you have, not the enjoyable one, because the easy task will pass and teach you nothing about whether the year was a good idea. Export everything you produce in the first two days so the work outlives whatever you decide.

Then check the exit clock. The windows differ by vendor and by how you paid, measured in days at some and hours at others, and the refund windows for all three vendors is the page that matters before the weekend closes. If the plan fails on fit rather than capacity, that clock is the only thing on your calendar worth watching.

If the window has already closed, the money is spent and the question changes. You own twelve months of a tool, so put the work it is good at through it and stop paying for a second plan that does the same job. The renewal date you just created is the one to write down.

What the Test Cannot Tell You

It cannot tell you whether the ceiling will still be there in March. Free limits move in both directions without notice, and a plan that fits in November can feel tight in February. It also says nothing about features that exist only above the paywall, which is why one of the three passes should use the exact format you need at work rather than a cleaner sample.

It cannot price your patience either. Two corrections per document is fine on paper and tiresome by the fifth, and no threshold in that table captures the drift.

The Verdict

Claude free won my test, and by my own rule that means it is enough for one 22-page agreement: two hand-fixes against a threshold of five, no wall, finished in one sitting. I would still pay the $20 monthly, because the correction round is the part of the job I resent and a cleaner first draft is what the paid tier actually sells.

ChatGPT free failed on the clock rather than the prose, so its $20 plan is a capacity purchase: worth it if the 9-message wall shows up twice a week, not worth it because one memo read well once. Gemini free failed quietly, which is the result I would least like to repeat. A tool that reads half your document and writes a confident summary of it is a tool to check, not one to tier up.

If your own four numbers look like mine, buy monthly rather than annual and let a month decide. The head-to-head on the two $20 plans is the next read if the choice is between them. If the numbers do not look like mine, you have just saved yourself a year of something.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is one day really enough to judge an AI tool?

For one task, yes. Three passes on a real document surface the two failures that decide a purchase: a message ceiling and a citation error. What a day cannot show you is how the tool behaves on the fifth document, which is the reason to buy monthly first and switch to annual only after a month of real use.

What if the free tier passes but I still want the paid plan?

Then you are buying capacity, and capacity is worth paying for only when the ceiling shows up on ordinary work. Count the wall hits. One on a bad afternoon is a prompt problem, two in a week is a subscription you will use. A $20 month answers that in 30 days, which is cheaper than being wrong about a $200 year.

Do AI subscriptions get cheaper on Black Friday?

On November 18, 2026, neither OpenAI nor Anthropic listed a holiday price on its US pricing page, and the Google plan carried its usual $19.99. Claude Pro at $200 for twelve months instead of $240 is a 16.7% saving available in any month, not a November event. Buying on the 27th gets you the number you can pay today.

I already bought an annual plan. What should I do first?

Run the hardest task you have on it today and export the result. Then check the vendor's exit window before the weekend ends, because the three big vendors measure it differently and the clock does not always start when you assume it does. If the plan fails on fit rather than capacity, that window is the only thing on the calendar that matters.

Which free tier should I test first?

The one whose paid plan you are actually considering, not the one with the best reviews. A free tier is a preview of a specific vendor's model and limits, so testing a tool you would never pay for tells you nothing about the purchase in front of you. If two plans are close, run the task on both and compare hand-fixes; that number moves more than benchmark scores do.
