ChatGPT vs Claude for Thanksgiving Seating: 14 Guests, 16 Seats, 10 Rules

Two tables, one 10-seat oval, ten rules that check each other, and one divorced couple who have not shared a table since 2019. Nine of ten rules each, 5 minutes 20 seconds against 14 minutes 48.

Sixteen chairs, fourteen people, ten rules that all have to hold at once.

The chart took longer to get right than the turkey will. A 20-pound bird is one number in one oven. A table for fourteen is ten numbers that check each other.

My sister's dining room in DeWitt, New York holds a 10-seat oval, and a 6-seat folding table goes up nine feet away in the living room. Both fill on Thursday, November 26, at 3:30. The guest list stopped moving on October 30. A seating chart is a constraint problem, and nothing can be solved while the list is still moving.

I ran the brief on Saturday morning, November 14, then tested every rule against the seat numbers on paper instead of reading the answer the way a guest would. That is how the one broken rule turned up.

Bottom line: both charts met nine of ten rules, and they broke different ones. ChatGPT printed a numbered chart in 2 minutes 20 seconds, seated Carol directly across from Ray, then fixed its own error in one line by moving Ray to the kids' table. Claude got the geometry right and handed back prose, which cost eleven minutes of drawing. 5 minutes 20 seconds against 14 minutes 48. Take ChatGPT when the table can absorb one move. Take Claude when moving Uncle Ray is not a conversation you want to have this year.

The Short Answer

ChatGPT, on the deliverable: nine rules on the first chart, a tenth once its own plan B was applied, and a layout my sister could use without editing it.

Claude reasoned better. It was the only one to notice that seats 3 and 9 face each other, which is the rule that needs counting rather than reading. Its failure was the format: ten described positions instead of ten numbers, and eleven minutes of my Saturday.

Two figures hold it: 5 minutes 20 seconds against 14 minutes 48 to a chart I trusted, and nine of ten rules each, on different rules.

The food is a separate problem. The oven arithmetic is in the dinner plan test, where the same two tools disagreed about a 20-pound bird, and the wider picture is in our full ChatGPT vs Claude comparison.

The Room Decides Half the Answer

A 10-seat oval has five facing pairs: 1 and 6 at the ends, then 2 and 10, 3 and 9, 4 and 8, 5 and 7. Number the seats clockwise from the kitchen door and the arithmetic becomes visible. Two people who cannot look at each other need a gap that is neither a neighbor nor a facing pair, and ten seats leave very few arrangements.

Three features of the room did most of the work. The kitchen door is beside seat 1. The sideboard, where the bird gets carved, is behind seat 1. And there is a one-inch threshold strip at the door end of the floor, which is exactly where a wheelchair should not go.

That settled both ends before either model wrote a word: Joe at seat 6, where the floor runs flat, and Doug at seat 1 with three feet of floor behind him for the knife.

What Claude Sent Back

Claude worked down the list in order and got every geometric rule right: Joe at 6, Doug at 1, Ruth at 10, Jen at 2, Ray at 4, Carol at 9.

It also caught the rule that needs counting. Seats 3 and 9 face each other, so Carol at 9 with Ray at 4 keeps four seats and a corner between them. Both models were told that Carol will not look at him for two hours. Only one of them checked which seats actually face.

Then it handed back ten bullet points: head of table, wall side, second from the door. Correct, and unusable at a table for sixteen chairs with four kids in the room. I drew the oval, numbered it and re-checked the names against the rules. Eleven minutes.

What ChatGPT Sent Back

A numbered chart, clockwise from the door: 1 Doug, 2 Jen, 3 Ray, 4 Maria, 5 Paul, 6 Joe, 7 Priya, 8 Tyler, 9 Carol, 10 Ruth.

Nine of the ten rules hold. The one that fails is easy to miss: Ray lands at 3 and Carol at 9, and those two face each other across the bird. It had copied the rule into its own checklist and then broken it while numbering.

The useful part came underneath. A second arrangement for a quieter table: move Ray to the kids' table, where two chairs sat empty and one 11-year-old would enjoy the company. That single line takes the chart to ten of ten, because a man at a different table is neither beside Carol nor across from her.

Applying it took three minutes, most of them spent deciding whether my mother would forgive me. She would. Ray would not.

Doing It in the Right Order

Saturday: write the list down with ages, and mark the people who cannot move. Here that was Joe, Doug and Ruth: a wheelchair, a carving knife and a hearing aid are not negotiable.

Then run the brief once, asking for seats numbered clockwise from a named landmark and for the empty chairs to be named too. If the model answers in positions, ask for numbers in the same chat, then check the five facing pairs yourself. That second pass beats redrawing the table by hand.

By Friday the 20th: print the chart with the name cards. The linen goes down that weekend, and a chart that arrives on the 25th is decoration.

The Verdict

Winner: ChatGPT. Nine rules on the first chart, ten with plan B applied, 5 minutes 20 seconds from send to a numbered sheet. The broken rule was real, but the fix sat in the same answer, which is the difference between a chart and an essay.

Claude is the one to use if the table cannot move. Its ten names sit correctly on a layout that satisfies every seating rule, including the facing-pair rule ChatGPT missed. Budget fifteen minutes for the drawing, and do not read the bullets as if they were the finished answer.

Two lines worth copying: name the facing pairs, and say how many chairs are empty and who may use them. Those two sentences separate a chart from a list of names.

Where this stops working: eleven adults and no kids' table removes the escape hatch, and then the facing-pair rule has one solution on a ten-seat oval. At that point neither model is doing the work. You are, with a pencil and a floor plan.
