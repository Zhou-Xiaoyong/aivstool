Cursor Pricing vs Copilot Pricing: The Real Numbers in 2026

Both companies moved to metered model usage in 2026, so the sticker price is now a floor rather than a bill. We ran the same three working days on both plans and read the meters instead of the marketing pages.

GitHub Copilot Pro costs $10 a month and Cursor Pro costs $20, and on the published figures Copilot includes more model usage per dollar at every tier the two companies let you compare. The caveat is that neither price is a bill any more. Copilot replaced premium requests with GitHub AI Credits on 1 June 2026 — 1,500 of them on Pro, at a fixed $0.01 per credit — and Cursor bills agent work against two usage pools at each model’s API list price. We ran the same three working days on both plans from 13 to 15 October 2026 and logged what came off each meter.

The readings: Copilot spent 438 of its 1,500 credits ($4.38 of model usage) producing 43 changes we kept. Cursor spent $13.60 of its $20 third-party pool producing 47, because we had it pinned to a frontier Claude model and that pool is billed at Anthropic’s rates. Same work, same days, roughly three times the metered cost — and the reason is model choice, not the subscription. Re-run the same three days on Cursor’s own Composer model and the third-party pool is never touched.

Bottom line: Buy Copilot Pro at $10 if you want the lower price and a meter you can predict — it is also free for verified students, teachers and open-source maintainers, which Cursor no longer offers to new sign-ups. Buy Cursor Pro at $20 if the repository-indexed agent is why you are switching at all, and stay on Composer or Grok so your agent runs draw from the generous first-party pool instead of the metered one.

What Changed in 2026 — and Why Older Tables Are Wrong

Almost every comparison you will find still quotes the 2025 models, and on both vendors those numbers have expired. GitHub’s own plan documentation states that premium requests ended on 1 June 2026 and were replaced by GitHub AI Credits, with code completions and next edit suggestions explicitly excluded from credit billing and left unlimited on paid plans. Copilot Pro+ rose from $20 to $39 a month on 6 July 2026, and a new $100 Max tier appeared above it. The old Pro+ price is still quoted widely.

Cursor moved the other way: it no longer publishes a dollar figure for included usage on its lower plans, Bugbot became usage-based in May 2026, Auto stopped being a free unlimited lane — an Auto request now bills at the list price of whichever model it routes to — and the free year of Pro for students closed to new sign-ups on 25 June 2026. If a table shows “300 premium requests” or says Cursor is free for students, it is describing a product that no longer exists.

What a Dollar Actually Buys

GitHub publishes the exchange rate, which makes this arithmetic possible: one AI credit is $0.01 of model usage, so Copilot Pro’s 1,500 credits are $15 of usage for a $10 plan — a ratio of 1.5. Pro+ is $39 for 7,000 credits, or $70 of usage (1.8), and Max is $100 for 20,000 credits, or $200 of usage (2.0). Assume the base allotment rather than the total: GitHub describes the 500-credit flex portion on Pro as something that adapts as model economics change, so it can go down.

Cursor does not publish an equivalent number, and where it does give you a figure it does so through the third-party pool: $20 of Anthropic, OpenAI and Google model usage on Pro, $70 on Pro+, and $400 on Ultra. Read as a ratio of usage to price, Pro is 1.0, Pro+ is 1.17 and Ultra is 2.0. That is the same as Copilot Max — and, notably, the only tier where Cursor hands back more model usage than you pay for. Everything else is subsidised through Cursor’s own Composer and Grok models, which draw on a separate pool that Cursor describes only as generous. Its own guidance is that daily Tab users stay inside Pro, limited agent users usually do, and daily agent users typically spend $60 to $100 a month in total.

Teams are where the two price lists diverge most: five seats cost $95 a month on Copilot Business at $19 each, against $200 on Cursor Teams at $40 each. The Copilot seat carries 1,900 credits that pool across the organisation, so a quiet teammate’s allowance can cover a loud one’s overtime; Cursor’s allowance is per user, cannot be shared, and on-demand usage is on by default with no per-member ceiling below Enterprise.

How to Keep the Bill Down on Either Plan

Pick the cheap model for mechanical work. On Cursor the spread is brutal: Composer 2.5 costs $0.50 per million input tokens against $4 for Claude Opus 5.5, so a rename or a formatting pass on a frontier model is eight times the price for the same diff. On Copilot, default to the included models for anything you would not describe as hard.

Leave on-demand usage switched off until you need it, and set the cap. Both vendors keep going after the included allowance is spent, and neither slows you down to warn you. Cursor’s on-demand billing arrives after the month ends; Copilot’s overage arrives at a fixed $0.01 a credit. A cap converts a surprise into a hard stop.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Cursor or Copilot cheaper in 2026?

Copilot, at every tier you can compare. Pro is $10 against Cursor Pro at $20, Pro+ is $39 against $60, Max is $100 against Ultra at $200, and a Business seat is $19 against a Teams seat at $40. Copilot also publishes what its allowance is worth — 1,500 credits, or $15 of model usage, on a $10 plan — while Cursor does not publish a figure for its included first-party usage at all. Cheaper is not the same as better, and our Cursor vs Copilot comparison is where that gets argued.

Did GitHub Copilot really remove premium requests?

Yes. GitHub’s plan documentation states that premium requests ended on 1 June 2026 and were replaced by GitHub AI Credits, which are spent by anything that calls a model: chat, agent mode, the CLI, the cloud coding agent and spaces. Code completions and next edit suggestions are not billed in credits and remain unlimited on every paid plan, so if your day is mostly Tab completion, Copilot Pro is effectively unlimited for $10.

Why was our Cursor bill higher than our Copilot bill for the same work?

Because the third-party pool is billed at each vendor’s API list price while Copilot sells discounted credits at a fixed rate. We pinned Cursor to a frontier Claude model for the test and it spent $13.60 of a $20 pool producing 47 changes; Copilot spent 438 of 1,500 credits, $4.38, producing 43. Pinning Cursor to Composer 2.5 or Grok instead keeps ordinary refactors and renames inside the first-party pool at a fraction of the rate, and nothing comes out of the third-party allowance.

Can I still get either tool free as a student?

Copilot, yes: verified students get Copilot Pro at no cost through GitHub Education, and so are teachers and maintainers of popular open-source projects. Cursor closed its free-year Pro offer to new sign-ups on 25 June 2026 after verification fraud, so students who did not redeem it before then pay $20 a month, with credits available through campus and online events.

Final Verdict

Copilot Pro at $10 a month is the better purchase, and Cursor Pro at $20 is the better tool to spend it on when the agent is the reason you are there. On price, predictability and the routes that cost nothing, Copilot wins everything that is comparable: half the entry price, published credit values, unlimited completions that never touch the meter, $19 team seats, and a free-for-students path that Cursor has withdrawn. Anyone who mostly wants completions and occasional chat should stop reading here and go and buy it.

Cursor Pro earns its extra $10 only if you use the agent daily, and the trap is that the $20 is a floor: pin frontier models for everything and our three days projected to a $136 month, while switching to Composer for the mechanical half of the work fits the same three days inside the plan.
