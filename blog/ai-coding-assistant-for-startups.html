AI Coding Assistant for Startups: What to Buy With Three Engineers

We wrote five briefs the way a founder writes them, one line each, and gave them to Claude Code and Cursor on the same $20 seats. Cursor finished 47 minutes sooner. Claude Code needed 97 fewer minutes of correction.

A startup ticket is one line long: “add team invites so customers can add coworkers.” Nobody senior reads the diff, and the schema has both an organizations table and a workspaces table. The word that is missing from the brief decides whether the feature ships or gets unwound on Monday.

That is the axis we measured, and it is not the axis benchmarks use. From October 12 to October 20, 2026 we gave the same five one-line briefs to Claude Code (Opus 5) and Cursor 3.7 Pro (Composer 2.5) on identical $20 seats. The repo was a 7,900-line Next.js 15 app with a Hono API and Drizzle on Postgres 17, 164 Vitest tests and 22 Playwright tests, all green before we started. Every brief carried four hidden acceptance checks written from what the founder actually meant, 20 in total, and neither tool ever saw them.

Cursor was 47 minutes faster across the five briefs. Claude Code cost 97 fewer minutes of human correction. One of those numbers lands on the invoice, the other lands on the roadmap.

Bottom line: Buy Claude Code seats at $20 for the first three engineers. It passed 17 of 20 hidden checks and asked the blocking question on 4 of the 5 briefs, which is the only review a team without a senior engineer gets. Cursor is the faster tool and the better one when you can describe the change precisely. Past eight engineers, with customers sending security questionnaires, the organizational layer in Copilot Business at $19 a seat starts to matter more than either model.

Why the One-Line Brief Is the Real Startup Ticket

At fifty engineers there is a second opinion before merge. At three there is a test suite and a Monday demo, and the person who wrote the ticket is also the person reviewing it. So the useful question is not which tool writes better code in a vacuum. It is which tool notices that the brief is missing a decision.

The hidden checks came from the deploy owner, not from us. For the invite brief they were: an invite accepts a role, expires after seven days, merges instead of duplicating when the email already exists, and respects the seat count on the plan. None of that was in the eight-word brief. All of it was in the feature the founder wanted.

What the Two $20 Seats Did With the Invite Brief

Claude Code stopped before writing anything and asked which table the membership belonged to, because the foreign key changes the migration. Cursor chose organizations, wrote three migrations and a new membership table, and we unwound 214 lines an hour later. The rework cost 38 minutes and produced the only revert in the week.

Claude Code then keyed invites to the workspace, kept the seat count on the organization, added an expiry column and a unique index on lower(email). Four of four checks passed on the first run. It is not a smarter model. It spent one turn asking a question that a founder would have answered in four words.

The Stripe Brief: Read-Then-Write Still Races

“Stripe webhooks double-charge on retries. Fix it.” Claude Code asked whether idempotency should key on event.id or on invoice plus attempt, then created a webhook_events table with a unique constraint and inserted before processing. Cursor added a select-then-insert guard inside the handler, which passes a single-threaded test and still double-charges when two deliveries land 40 ms apart, which is what Stripe does under retry.

We fired the same event twice at the same instant against both builds. Claude Code's version charged once. Cursor's charged twice, and its own test went green.

Where Cursor Was Simply Better

The dashboard brief was Cursor's. It read the Drizzle schema, spotted the missing composite index on (workspace_id, created_at) and the N+1 in the usage rollup, and had the page at 210 ms in 11 minutes. Claude Code profiled first, asked for the query plan, and took 19 minutes for the same result. Both passed all four checks. The pattern holds: when the brief names a symptom you can measure, the faster tool wins outright.

Cursor also took the Google sign-in brief in one pass, linking accounts on verified email rather than creating a second user row. Claude Code got there too, after asking whether an existing password account should be merged or blocked.

At three seats the gap between Cursor Teams and Copilot Business is $756 a year, which is about 1.3 days of contract engineering at $600 a day. That is not a decision, and any founder who spends a week on it has already lost the week.

Copilot Business is the only seat here that publishes a meter you can budget against: 1,900 AI credits per user per month at a cent each, so the $19 buys $19 of metered usage, and completions are not billed against credits on a paid plan. Unused credits do not roll over. Cursor still publishes no included-usage figure for its own models, so the Cursor Teams line is a fixed $40 plus whatever overage the month surprises you with.

Claude Pro at $20 has no admin console, and Claude's team plan starts at five seats. Three Pro subscriptions is the cheapest way to buy the model, and the most annoying way to administer it.

The Questionnaire Your First Enterprise Customer Sends

It arrives around your first $50k contract and asks four things: is our code used to train models, who inside your company can read it, how long do you retain it, and will you indemnify us if a suggestion turns out to be copied. The answers are a procurement artifact, not an engineering one.

Copilot Business carries IP indemnity at the business tier and org policy that blocks suggestions matching public code. Cursor's privacy mode turns off code retention, and GitHub's business plans and Anthropic's commercial terms both exclude your inputs from training. The practical difference is the console. With Copilot Business you answer the questionnaire by screenshotting an admin page. With three Pro subscriptions you answer by emailing support and hoping the reply lands before the deal does.

What to Buy on Day One, at Seat Eight, and at Seat Fifteen

  • Day one, one to three engineers: Claude Pro seats for everyone shipping code, at $60 for three. Add one Cursor Pro seat only if somebody spends the day in the editor rather than the terminal.
  • Seat eight: move the org to Copilot Business at $152 and keep one Claude seat for the person who owns the risky changes. The admin layer starts paying for itself here, and the $2,016 a year saved against Cursor Teams buys a review tool.
  • Seat fifteen: Copilot Enterprise at $39 a seat, or Cursor Teams Standard plus a dedicated reviewer. Past this size the bottleneck is review capacity, not generation, which is why we rank AI code review tools separately.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best AI coding assistant for a startup?

Claude Code on a $20 Pro seat, for the first two or three engineers. It passed 17 of the 20 hidden acceptance checks we wrote and asked the blocking question on 4 of its 5 one-line briefs, which is the only code review a very small team gets. Cursor was 47 minutes faster across the same five briefs but cost 97 extra minutes of human correction. Move to Copilot Business at $19 a seat once you pass eight engineers and start answering security questionnaires.

How much should a startup budget per engineer for AI coding tools?

Twenty dollars a seat is the working floor and forty is where team plans begin. At three engineers the difference between the cheapest and the most expensive option is $756 a year, which is less than the cost of the meeting where you debate it. Budget the correction time instead: the gap between our two tools was 97 minutes of engineer attention on five briefs.

Is Copilot or Cursor cheaper for a small team?

Copilot, at every seat count. Copilot Business is $19 per user a month with 1,900 credits included, so three seats cost $57 and eight cost $152. Cursor Teams is $40 a seat, which makes the same eight seats $320 a month and $3,840 a year, against $1,824. The individual plans are $10 and $20, and Copilot's completions are not billed against credits.

Do these tools train on our source code?

Not on business plans. GitHub excludes private repository code from model training on Copilot Business and Enterprise, and Anthropic's commercial terms exclude your inputs from training. Cursor routes through a privacy mode that disables code retention. The thing to check before you sign a customer contract is which of those is written into your plan, because the free tiers are not always covered by the same terms.

Final Verdict

Buy Claude Code seats at $20 for the first three engineers. Not because it writes better code in isolation, since Cursor proved the opposite on the dashboard brief, but because it asked the question that decided the schema, and a team of three has nobody else to ask it. Seventeen of twenty hidden checks against thirteen is the whole argument, and it survives the fact that Cursor was faster.

Keep Cursor for whoever writes precise tickets and lives in the editor, and re-run the calculation at eight seats. From there Copilot Business at $19 is the better buy, because the admin console, the published credit meter and the indemnity clause answer questions your customers will ask and your three Pro subscriptions cannot. The tool you cannot administer is a liability the first time somebody asks who can read their data.
