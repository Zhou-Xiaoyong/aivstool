Claude vs Gemini for Thanksgiving Leftovers: 24 Slots, 19 Portions

Ninety-eight ounces off an 18-pound bird is 19 five-ounce portions, not the 24 a household needs. One plan counted that. The other fed the bird through Tuesday.

Ninety-eight ounces of carved turkey is 19 portions at five ounces each. Three adults eating lunch and dinner from Friday through Monday need 24.

That gap of five is the only hard part of Thanksgiving leftovers, and it is not the question either assistant was asked. Both were handed the same brief. One of them did the division.

Dinner was Thursday, November 26, 2026. An 18-pound bird went into a 325°F oven at noon and came out at 4:40. After it rested I carved 6 pounds 2 ounces off it, weighed on a kitchen scale, and everything below runs on that 98 ounces, a refrigerator at 38°F, and a freezer with a shelf free.

Bottom line: follow Gemini's calendar. It counted 19 portions at the five ounces the brief specified, put Monday on day five of a three-to-four-day window, and moved 30 ounces to the freezer on Thursday night. Claude cooked better food: five dishes with real oven times, and a covered-dish reheat that brings sliced breast back without drying it. It also had us eating turkey on Tuesday, December 1, which is day six, and it never saw the five-portion gap because it counted four ounces a portion after being told five.

The Short Answer

Cooked turkey keeps 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator and 3 to 4 months in the freezer, and it goes back on the plate at 165°F. Cooked on Thursday the 26th, the refrigerator window closes on Sunday the 29th. Monday's dinner comes out of the freezer or off the stove, and there is no third option.

Both models had those dates in the prompt. One applied them to the calendar. The cook-day test covers the oven and the thaw; this page covers the four days after. If you are still deciding what to pay for at all, the Thanksgiving week buying guide starts there, and the travel half of the holiday got its own timed test.

Day Five Is Where the Window Closes

The USDA counts storage from the day the food was cooked, not from the first day somebody ate it cold. Thursday is day one. That makes Sunday day four and Monday day five.

Claude counted Friday as day one. Under that arithmetic Monday is day four, still inside the window, so its plan serves the last of the bird on Monday night and turkey sandwiches on Tuesday. Tuesday is December 1. That is day six.

One day of counting, and it decides whether Monday dinner is leftovers or a risk. It is also the kind of error that shows up when a model treats a rule as a fact worth mentioning instead of a number worth running. Claude mentioned the window twice and never subtracted.

98 Ounces Is 19 Portions, Not 24

An 18-pound bird weighs 288 ounces raw. Mine gave up 98 ounces of meat, so the carve ran 34% of the purchase. The other 66% is carcass, skin, and whatever stayed in the pan.

At five ounces a portion, 98 ounces is 19 portions, with 3 ounces of trim left for the stock pot. A recipe that says a turkey "serves 12" is counting the whole bird at a bigger slice, and it is not counting the same thing you are.

Sunday is where the plan bends. Twelve portions go on Friday and Saturday, which leaves one for Sunday lunch and nothing for Sunday dinner. The last day inside the window is also the first day the kitchen has to produce something that is not turkey.

Thirty ounces flat-frozen in six quart bags on Thursday night sits in a corner of the shelf, thaws in the fridge in a day, and arrives on Monday with four days of margin.

What to Buy Before November 25

Nobody should be in a grocery store on the Wednesday before Thanksgiving or the Friday after. Everything on this list is a Tuesday purchase, and none of it is food you would not use anyway.

Two inches of depth is the number that matters, and it is why a set of tall 12-cup containers is the wrong purchase. A whole breast packed into one deep tub stays warm in the middle long after the outside reads cold, and warm in the middle is exactly what the two-hour rule is about. The USDA asks for shallow containers and sliced meat for the same reason.

What to Skip

The 30-piece container set. By January half the lids will not match half the bases, and none of the tall ones stack flat in a fridge that also holds a quart of stock.

A vacuum sealer does not earn its counter space for four days of storage. A second roasting pan is a purchase for a household that hosts twice, which is a different problem with a different budget. And a leftover-recipe book solves the part that was never difficult, which is what to cook.

What to throw out is shorter than most guides suggest. Skin, which does not survive a reheat, the picked carcass once the stock is strained, and anything that sat on the table past two hours. Our kitchen ran at 68°F that evening, so the two-hour rule applied; the one-hour version is for a room above 90°F, which is a summer picnic and not a November dining room.

The Gravy and the Carcass

Gravy is a liquid, and the USDA wants gravies brought to a rolling boil rather than warmed through. Claude said to reheat it gently, which is how you end up with a sauce that is hot at the rim of the pan and cool in the middle. Gemini said boil. Boil is right, and it takes about four minutes from cold.

The carcass is where Claude wins outright. It broke the bird down, covered it with cold water in a 6-quart pot with an onion and two bay leaves, simmered four hours, then strained and reduced to 3 quarts. Gemini suggested 90 minutes, which produces turkey-flavored water and a second round of picking through bones.

Where Claude Wins

Five dishes, each with an oven temperature and a time: a pot pie, a rice soup built on that stock, a sheet-pan hash, quesadillas, and a covered-dish reheat of sliced breast at 300°F for 12 minutes with a splash of stock. That covered-dish line is the best single thing either model wrote. Sliced breast reheated uncovered goes chalky in eight minutes; under a lid with moisture it comes back close to Thursday.

Gemini wrote four dishes and put quesadillas on the table twice. Its food is thinner, and its buy-ahead list runs six items against nine, with a roasting pan sitting on the wrong one.

On the model-level question, the Claude vs Gemini document test lands in the same place: Gemini holds a structure, Claude holds a voice.

The Verdict

Winner: Gemini. It counted the portions at the size the prompt specified, put Monday on day five, froze 30 ounces on Thursday night, and named the five slots turkey could not fill. Follow its calendar and you will not throw away Monday's dinner on Tuesday.

Take Claude's kitchen. The recipes, the covered-dish reheat, and the four-hour stock are better than anything in the other plan. Ignore its dates while you cook from it.

The rule worth keeping: divide carved ounces by five, multiply people by meals by days, and freeze the difference on the night you cook. Here that is 98 divided by 5 against 3 times 2 times 4, and the 30 ounces that close the gap go into the freezer before the dishes are done.

Where this stops applying: a 12-pound bird for two people leaves no gap at all, and eight people with a chest freezer have the opposite problem. The arithmetic holds in both cases. Only the answer changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do Thanksgiving leftovers last in the refrigerator?

Three to four days for cooked turkey, counting from the day it was cooked. A bird roasted on Thursday, November 26 is good through Sunday the 29th and out of the refrigerator window on Monday the 30th. Cooked turkey also freezes for three to four months, and every reheated portion should reach 165°F.

Can you eat leftover turkey on the Monday after Thanksgiving?

Yes, if it went into the freezer on Thursday or Friday and thawed in the refrigerator. Monday, November 30 is day five for a bird cooked on the 26th, one day past the three-to-four-day refrigerator window. Thaw six portions in the fridge from Sunday morning, then reheat to 165°F. Turkey that stayed in the fridge since Thursday should be discarded.

How many portions does an 18-pound turkey actually give?

Ours carved out at 98 ounces, which is 19 portions at five ounces each. That is 34% of the raw weight, because the rest is carcass, skin and pan drippings. Most plans overestimate here: they count the bird, not the plate.

Can you reheat leftover gravy from Thanksgiving?

Yes, and it should come to a rolling boil rather than a gentle warm, because gravy is a liquid and the middle of a saucepan heats last. Four minutes from cold is typical for a quart. Gravy that sat out of the refrigerator for more than two hours should be thrown away instead of boiled.
